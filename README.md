# AI & Data Science Field Journal

A growing collection of visual study guides I make while learning AI, machine learning, data science, mathematics, systems, and related topics.

I started creating these while working through my University of Michigan MADS program, the Johns Hopkins Agentic AI program, courses from Coursera and DeepLearning.AI, academic papers, technical books, and hands-on projects.

The problem was that they captured **what I learned**, but not always **how I came to understand it**.

Some of the ideas I most want to remember come from working through a concept from several angles: connecting material across courses, reasoning through questions with an LLM, reconciling different explanations, building examples, and figuring out the mental model that makes the pieces fit together.

That's the part I want to preserve.

These Field Journals are first and foremost for future me: something I can return to 6, 12, or 18 months later and use to quickly reconstruct an idea without starting over from a pile of notes.

But I'm also sharing them in the hope that other curious people find them useful, engaging, and maybe even a fun way to follow along with the learning journey.

## Why Field Journals?

Months from now, I probably won't remember every derivation, implementation detail, or lecture.

A year from now, some of the courses and videos may not even be accessible to me anymore.

So these are designed as **rapid-recall tools**.

They focus on the ideas that felt most useful, interesting, durable, or worth understanding deeply:

- the mental model behind a technique
- why an architecture is designed the way it is
- relationships between ideas that are often taught separately
- practical examples that make an abstract concept tangible
- distinctions that are easy to forget
- the reasoning behind a framework, paper, or method

They are not meant to replace textbooks, papers, courses, or detailed notes.

They are meant to help me find my way back into an idea quickly — and hopefully help someone else find their way into it too.

## Why visual?

I read a lot of comic books as a kid and still enjoy the occasional graphic novel.

There's something powerful about being able to move through a complicated idea visually — almost like reading a graphic novel on a single page.

Diagrams can preserve relationships that are awkward to reconstruct from paragraphs alone. A visual sequence can remind me not only what the components are, but how they interact and why they matter.

That makes the format especially useful for the kinds of things I'm usually studying: neural network architectures, mathematical transformations, data workflows, reasoning frameworks, and systems.

## How they are made

The process usually starts with source material: coursework, papers, books, lectures, notebooks, or projects.

Then I work through the topic with an LLM — asking questions, testing explanations, connecting it to things I've learned elsewhere, and trying to identify the smallest set of ideas that would let me reconstruct the larger concept later.

The final infographic is the compressed artifact of that process.

The goal is not to preserve everything.

It's to preserve enough of the **reasoning, structure, and mental model** that I can rebuild the rest.

## Design

The visual style is inspired in part by two artists I’ve loved for very different reasons: David Roberts, whose 19th-century lithographs of Egypt and the Near East I used to pore over, and J. M. W. Turner, whose landscapes I loved discovering at the Yale Center for British Art. Roberts brings the sense of expedition, ruins, scale, and observation; Turner brings atmosphere, light, and motion. British Art at Yale
The Field Journal style borrows from both: part scientific expedition notebook, part modern technical publication.

The recurring question behind each page is:

> **Will future me understand this two/three/five years from now?**

If the collection also helps other people explore these ideas in a more visual, approachable way, even better.
